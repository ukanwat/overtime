# The world inventory — hundreds of KINDS of thing, not hundreds of buildings

A GTA-scale map is not a set of buildings on a terrain. Buildings are maybe a fifth of what
a player actually sees. The rest is infrastructure, utilities, transport, industry,
agriculture, nature, street furniture, signage, vehicles and life — and it is the *variety of
kinds* that makes a world read as real, not the count of instances.

Use this as a catalogue to steal from and extend, **not** a checklist to tick. If your world
contains only what is listed here, you have under-delivered: real cities are stranger and more
specific than any list. **There is no number to reach and no ceiling — every additional kind of
real thing makes the world better**, so keep widening the vocabulary for as long as you have
time. The only condition is that each thing goes where its real counterpart would actually be,
because a hydrant in a field is worse than no hydrant.

Keep a tally in `WORLD_INVENTORY.md`: kinds per category, and which district each appears in.
When a category has three entries, you have found your next session's work.

## How to place it, so it isn't set dressing
- **Everything belongs to a system.** Cables run from pylons to substations to poles to
  buildings. Bins are where a lane meets a kitchen door. Hydrants are on street corners at
  regular spacing. A crane serves a berth. If an object has no plausible reason to be at
  that spot, it reads as decoration and costs you realism instead of adding it.
- **Cluster, don't scatter.** Real clutter accumulates: against walls, in corners, behind
  buildings, along fences, where nobody tidies. Evenly-spread props look sprinkled.
- **Age it by place.** The same object should be newer downtown and rusted at the docks.
- **Repeat with variation, and let the variations tell you where you are.** A district's
  identity is as much its bin type, pole type, sign font and graffiti tag as its buildings.

## Verify
Fly the map and, per district, list every kind of object you can see that is not a building,
a road or terrain — and keep listing until you run out. If you run out quickly, that district
is empty, and the list you failed to finish is your next session's work.

# Making generated things read as real

Reference material, not instructions. Nothing here is a step to follow and nothing
here is a requirement — **you decide what your city is and you build every part of it
yourself.** Use what helps and invent past the rest.

## Land use comes before buildings

In a real city most parcels are not a building. If a generator's parcels can only become
buildings, no amount of tuning will produce a park, and the map will read as fill. A
vocabulary to draw on:

> park · plaza · square · cemetery · sports pitch · running track · courts · school yard ·
> surface car park · multi-storey car park · container yard · scrap yard · timber yard ·
> storage lot · bus depot · rail yard · sidings · quarry · substation · water treatment ·
> gas holder · silos · marina · boatyard · dry dock · fish market · allotments · nursery ·
> orchard · scrub · wetland · reed bed · dunes · cliff · bare rock · golf course ·
> fairground · drive-in · construction site · demolished lot · waste ground · drainage canal ·
> reservoir · helipad · airfield apron · fenced compound

How much of the land should be unbuilt? However much a real place has — look at aerial
photographs and count. There is no quota; more kinds of specific non-building land is better.

Density is a gradient with a peak, not a fill: one dense core wall-to-wall, then visibly
thinning — bigger parcels, gaps, yards — then edges that are mostly not city at all. A player
should be able to guess where they are in the city from density alone.

Singletons carry a map. A stadium, a power station, a cathedral, a row of container cranes, a
refinery, a prison, an airport terminal, a hilltop cemetery, a fairground wheel — each
appearing once, each visible from far off, each a thing players navigate by. If everything in
the skyline is a variation of the same tower, there is no skyline.

Pacing: drive in any direction for half a minute and something should change — the use of the
land, the grain of the blocks, the width of the road, a bridge, a tunnel, a crest, a plaza, a
sudden view of water. Worlds without pacing are wallpaper however large they are.

And it is a game, not a diorama: crests that hide the road, tunnels, hairpin descents, a
waterfront straight where you can hold top speed, an alley too narrow for a car, a spiral car
park you can drive to the roof of, jumpable gaps, dock ramps, connected rooftops.

## Authorship, not variation

Give a parcel a year and a builder before you give it a shape, and let those decide the
geometry. Independent construction systems — each with its own storey height, window
proportion, structural bay, roof, entrance, material and way of ageing — do what sliders
cannot:

> timber-frame walk-up · load-bearing brick with stone lintels · deco stucco with stepped
> parapets · mid-century curtain wall · brutalist precast · 1980s mirror glass on a podium ·
> contemporary rainscreen with stacked balconies · steel shed with translucent panels ·
> informal self-built accretion

The test is hard: if a stranger can see two neighbouring buildings and tell they came from the
same generator, the generator is too narrow.

Then let the city's history show. The block where the grid changes angle because it was
annexed later · the elevated road driven through a neighbourhood, darkening the street beneath
· the waterfront that was industry and is now condos with one crane kept as decor · the deco
strip with ugly infill where the originals burned · the field boundary that is now a road
meeting the grid at a stupid angle. Each costs one decision and no generator invents them by
accident.

And let individual buildings have life stories: an added floor that doesn't match · blocked-up
windows · a modern shopfront punched into an old frame · a fire escape bolted on · ducting
climbing a facade · half-repainted where a neighbour was demolished · the ghost of a roofline
on a party wall · two shops subdivided out of one with clashing signage · a hoarding where a
building is missing.

## Chaos that reads as real

Independent per-building randomness produces noise, which is its own kind of fake — a street
where everything differs in every way looks like a sample library. Real cities are coherent at
the building scale and chaotic at the block scale: one developer built this terrace in one
year so it repeats, and the next street is from a different decade so the change is abrupt.
Correlate era, builder and material per block or per terrace; vary only small things per
building.

More axes beat wider ranges — era, builder wealth, construction system, plot width and depth,
setback, storey height, ground-floor use, upper-floor use, roof type, state of repair,
additions, tenancy, signage age, paint generation, and any others you think of. Long-tailed
distributions rather than uniform ones. Genuinely rare things: a burnt-out shell, a church
wedged between towers, the one house that refused to sell now ringed by a car park, a
collapsed roof, a gable-sized mural, a boat in a front yard, a tree growing out of a chimney.

Break the grid on purpose — a diagonal rail alignment slicing blocks into triangles, a
culverted stream a street follows for no visible reason, a road that bends around something
no longer there, a dead end that used to be a through road.

And let systems collide. Where the motorway meets the old town, where the port meets housing,
where money meets no money — the seams are the most interesting places in any real city, and a
generator that treats districts as separate tiles never produces them.

## The big infrastructure

What players remember, and what a lazy generator skips: an airport with real runway and
taxiway markings, apron, terminal, tower, hangars, fuel farm, approach lighting — and a small
grass airfield somewhere too. A motorway system with elevated sections, a multi-level
interchange, merge tapers, gantries, barriers, a tunnel. Roads that are genuinely complicated:
one-way systems, filter lanes, box junctions, staggered crossroads, hairpins, an avenue that
changes width, a street too narrow for two cars, service alleys behind the shops. Heavy
industry: refinery pipework, tank farms, a flare stack, cooling towers, pipe bridges, rail
sidings, a haul road, a quarry, a power station. A working port: quay, container cranes,
stacks, straddle carriers, a RoRo ramp, a dry dock, moored ships. Rail: a yard with sidings
and wagons, a station with platforms and canopies, a viaduct, a level crossing.

# The real world's own taxonomy — and how to query it yourself

You do not have to imagine what exists. OpenStreetMap is millions of people cataloguing the
real world, and its **taginfo API is a frequency-ranked list of what is actually out there** —
no key, no auth:

```bash
curl -s "https://taginfo.openstreetmap.org/api/4/key/values?key=shop&rp=100&sortname=count_all&sortorder=desc" \
  | python3 -c "import json,sys;[print(v['value'],v['count']) for v in json.load(sys.stdin)['data']]"
```

Swap `key=` for any category: `shop` `amenity` `man_made` `leisure` `landuse` `craft`
`tourism` `historic` `power` `railway` `aeroway` `barrier` `natural` `waterway` `emergency`
`military` `office` `healthcare` `public_transport` `building` `highway` `sport` `advertising`
`telecom` `pipeline` `seamark`. Results come back ordered by how often each thing occurs in
reality, so the top of the list is what a street actually has and the tail is where the
one-off oddities live.

**The scale is the point.** Real OSM data contains **11,205 distinct kinds of shop, 9,454
kinds of amenity, 4,103 kinds of historic thing, 3,641 man-made structures, 3,173 crafts,
2,542 kinds of barrier, 2,143 land uses, 1,739 leisure facilities.** No brief can list that
and no generator needs all of it — but it should tell you how far from "buildings, roads and
trees" the real world is, and it is a bottomless source of specificity when a district feels
thin. Below is the top of each list, verbatim from real data.

# Kinds of people

A crowd reads as real when the people in it are visibly *doing different things for different
reasons*, not when there are many of them. Silhouette, pace, clothing and what they carry do
almost all the work — a figure with a ladder over one shoulder is legible at fifty metres and a
generic pedestrian is not, however good the mesh.

**By what they are doing right now:** commuting fast with headphones · waiting and checking a
phone · queueing at a bus stop or a club door · loading a van with the hazards on · unloading

**By district and hour** — the mix should flip, and that flip is worth more than extra models:

**Behaviour beats fidelity.** People should be going somewhere, cluster the way real crowds
do — bunching at crossings, thinning mid-block, in twos and threes with the occasional loner at
the edge — pause, look, step aside, and react to a car mounting the kerb. A well-behaved
low-poly crowd reads as real; a beautiful crowd walking through each other on rails does not.

**Ownership is realism, and repetition here is correct.** Real cities are full of the *same*
company's vans: one plumbing firm, one courier, one security company, one supermarket's lorries,
one municipal fleet livery, one taxi company's colours — appearing all over the map. Invent a
handful of fleets and repeat them everywhere; that reads as a functioning economy, whereas a
hundred unique unbranded vans reads as a game.
